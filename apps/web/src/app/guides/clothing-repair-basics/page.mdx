# Basic Clothing Repairs

Most of what shortens a garment's life is small and fixable: a button falls
off, a hem drops, a favourite jumper starts to pill. None of these need real
sewing skill. Here is how to fix all three by hand, from a completely
standing start.

## Sewing on a button

**What you need:** a needle, thread that matches the garment (or the button),
the button itself, small scissors, and a toothpick or matchstick.

1. **Thread the needle.** Cut about 40–50cm of thread. Push both ends through
   the eye of the needle so the needle sits in the middle of the thread, then
   tie the two loose ends together in a knot. You are now sewing with a
   doubled, stronger strand.
2. **Position the button.** Line it up where it used to sit, using the leftover
   thread marks or small holes in the fabric as a guide. Hold it in place, or
   use a pin to keep it steady.
3. **Anchor the thread.** From the underside (wrong side) of the fabric, push
   the needle through to the front and up through one hole of the button. This
   hides your starting knot underneath, out of sight.
4. **Leave room for a shank.** Lay a toothpick or matchstick flat across the
   top of the button, between the two holes you are working through. Sewing
   over the top of it keeps your stitches slightly loose.
5. **Stitch through the holes.** Push the needle down through the opposite
   hole, through the fabric, and back up through the first hole. Repeat this
   five or six times. For a four-hole button, do one pair of holes fully, then
   the other pair, so the stitches form a neat parallel or diagonal pattern.
6. **Build the shank.** Remove the toothpick. Bring the needle up between the
   button and the fabric, not through a hole, then wind the thread around the
   stitches underneath the button three or four times. This gap, called the
   shank, gives the button room to sit through a buttonhole without crushing
   the fabric.
7. **Finish off.** Push the needle through to the wrong side of the fabric,
   make two or three small stitches on the same spot to lock the thread, then
   trim it close to the fabric.

## Fixing a fallen hem

A dropped hem on trousers, a skirt or a dress is one of the most common
secondhand fixes, and it is done with a single stitch called a slip stitch,
which stays invisible from the outside.

**What you need:** a needle, thread that matches the garment, pins, small
scissors, and an iron if you have one.

1. **Turn the garment inside out.** You will be working from the back of the
   fabric the whole way through.
2. **Re-fold the hem** along its original crease line. If you have an iron,
   press the fold lightly; it makes the line much easier to follow and keeps
   the hem even.
3. **Pin the hem** every few centimetres to stop it shifting while you sew.
4. **Thread the needle** with a single strand this time, roughly 50–60cm long,
   and knot one end.
5. **Hide your first stitch.** Bury the knot inside the fold of the hem, then
   bring the needle out through the folded edge so the knot cannot be seen from
   either side.
6. **Work the slip stitch along the hem.** Pick up just a couple of threads
   from the main garment fabric, directly opposite where your needle came out,
   without pushing all the way through the fabric. Then slide the needle back
   into the fold of the hem and run it forward about half a centimetre inside
   the fold before bringing it out again. Pull the thread gently, not tight,
   and repeat this pattern along the whole hem: a small bite of the main
   fabric, then a slide through the fold.
7. **Finish off** with two or three small locking stitches inside the fold,
   then trim the thread. Turn the garment right-side out and check the front:
   a correctly worked slip stitch should be invisible or very close to it.

Give the hem a gentle tug once you are done. It should hold with no give,
which means it is ready to wear.

## Removing pilling from a jumper

Pilling is the small fuzzy balls that build up on a fabric's surface from
everyday friction, most common on wool knits, and on synthetics and blends.
It looks worse than it is: the fabric underneath is usually fine, and pilling
can be shaved off without damaging the garment.

**Tools, from gentlest to most aggressive:**

- **Fabric shaver** (small, battery-powered, with a guarded rotating blade).
  The fastest and safest option for most people, and inexpensive to buy.
- **Sweater comb** (a fine wire-mesh comb). More manual effort, but very
  precise, so it is a good choice for delicate or expensive knits.
- **Sweater stone** (a pumice-like block). Effective on sturdier knits, but
  more abrasive, so use it with a light hand on anything fine or loosely
  knitted.

**How to de-pill with a fabric shaver:**

1. Lay the garment flat on a hard surface and smooth out any folds or
   wrinkles.
2. Test a small hidden area first, such as an inside hem or under the arm, to
   make sure the tool does not damage that particular fabric.
3. Glide the shaver lightly over the pilled area in slow, gentle circular
   motions. Let the blades do the work; pressing hard is how you end up with
   a thin patch or a small hole rather than a smooth finish.
4. Empty the shaver's collection chamber as it fills, so the blades keep
   working freely.
5. When you are done, shake or brush the garment to remove any loose fibres.

**Using a comb or stone instead:** pull the fabric taut with one hand, then run
the comb or stone across the surface in one direction with light, even
pressure, working in small sections rather than the whole garment at once.

**Common mistakes:** pressing too hard, skipping the hidden-area test on a
fabric you are not sure about, and using an old razor blade instead of a
proper de-pilling tool, which carries a much higher risk of cutting the fabric
itself.

**Preventing pilling in the first place:** washing synthetics and loose knits
inside out, on a gentle cycle, and apart from rougher fabrics like denim or
towelling cuts down on the friction that causes pilling. See our
[fabric care guide](/guides/fabric-care-guide/) for the full washing routine
per fabric.
