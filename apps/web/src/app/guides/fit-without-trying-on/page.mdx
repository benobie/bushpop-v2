# Get the Right Fit Without Trying It On

Buying secondhand and vintage online means no fitting room. The good news is you
do not need one. A tape measure, five minutes, and your own numbers will get you a
far more reliable fit than trusting the size printed on the label. This guide
walks through how to measure yourself properly, and how to use those numbers to
judge whether a secondhand listing will actually fit.

## Why size labels lie

A size 12 today is not the same size 12 it was in the 1990s, and it is rarely the
same size 12 across two different brands even now. A few reasons secondhand and
vintage sizing is less reliable than it looks:

- **Brands size differently.** There is no single Australian or international
  standard that every label follows, so the same number means different actual
  measurements from one brand to the next.
- **Vintage runs its own way.** Clothing cut in past decades was generally sized
  to different standards than modern clothing, so an older labelled size can fit
  quite differently to the same number today.
- **Fabric changes with age and wash.** Natural fibres can shrink, knits can
  stretch out, and elastic loses its recovery over years of wear. Two examples of
  the same garment can end up measuring slightly differently.

The fix for all three is the same: measure, don't guess.

## How to measure yourself

Use a soft fabric tape measure if you have one. Wear light, close-fitting
clothing, stand naturally, and keep the tape snug against your body without
pulling it tight. A second pair of hands makes this easier, but it is fine to do
alone in front of a mirror.

| Measurement | How to take it |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Bust / chest | Wrap the tape around the fullest part of your chest, keeping it level and parallel to the floor. |
| Waist | Wrap the tape around your natural waist, roughly level with your belly button, at the narrowest point. |
| Hip | Wrap the tape around the fullest part of your hips and bottom. |
| Shoulder width | Measure straight across your back from the edge of one shoulder to the other. |
| Sleeve length | Measure from the edge of your shoulder, down the outside of your arm, to your wrist. |
| Inseam | Measure from your crotch straight down the inside of your leg to your ankle. |
| Total length (tops/dresses) | Measure from the top of your shoulder straight down to where you want the hem to sit. |

Write these numbers down somewhere you can find them again. They do not change
often, so this is a five-minute job that pays off on every secondhand purchase
you make afterwards.

## Comparing yourself to a listing

Once you have your own measurements, the next step is comparing them to the
garment, not the size label. Sellers who measure their items flat and list the
numbers are giving you exactly what you need. Our size charts guide has a full
[how to measure a garment](/guides/size-charts/#how-to-measure-a-garment) section,
including diagrams, that shows exactly where each garment measurement is taken.

A simple way to compare:

1. **Match the measurement type.** Line up your chest measurement against the
   garment's bust/chest measurement, your waist against its waist, and so on.
2. **Think about ease.** A garment's measurement does not need to match your body
   exactly. A close, fitted style might sit only a couple of centimetres larger
   than your body measurement; a relaxed or oversized style is meant to have more
   room. Check the listing photos and description for how the piece is meant to
   sit.
3. **Prioritise the measurement that matters most for that garment.** Bust or
   chest and shoulder width are usually the hardest to alter, so treat those as
   the most important checks on a top or jacket. Waist and length are more
   forgiving and easier to adjust with a hem or a belt.
4. **When in doubt, ask.** If a listing is missing a measurement you need, a
   quick message to the seller asking for it is completely normal and most
   sellers are glad to help.

## A benchmark garment is your best tool

The single best trick for buying secondhand online is keeping a benchmark: pick a
garment you already own that fits the way you like, measure it flat the same way
a seller would, and keep those numbers on hand. Comparing a new listing to a
garment you know fits well is far more reliable than comparing it to a size label,
especially once vintage and international sizing come into play.
